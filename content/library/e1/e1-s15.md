# From Particles to Fields to **Wannier Functions**

*A self-contained study guide following Altland & Simons, Ch. 1–2, built entirely toward understanding the Wannier basis.*

Study notes — Raju Sharma Khatiwada

---

## How to read these notes

Everything in Parts I and II is a *story*: a fast, honest reconstruction of Chapters 1 and 2 of Altland & Simons, kept only as deep as needed so that when we arrive at Wannier functions (Part III) nothing feels pulled out of thin air. From Part III onward the story mode stops: we go slowly, intuitively, and with full mathematics, at the level the book expects. Every section answers one question and hands its result to the next section.

> **Key idea (the single destination of the whole document).**
> A **Wannier function** is the lattice Fourier transform of a Bloch band. It is the *localized, orthonormal, complete* single-particle basis that turns a crystal (or any lattice model) into a collection of "sites" with hopping amplitudes. It is the mathematical justification for every tight-binding, Hubbard, and lattice-fermion Hamiltonian you have ever written down — including lattice models of monitored fermion circuits, where the "site operators" $c_i, c_i^\dagger$ are, implicitly, operators in a Wannier-type localized basis.

# Part I — The story of Chapter 1: why fields at all?

## 1. One idea, told through the harmonic chain

Chapter 1 of Altland & Simons makes a single point, three times (classically, quantum mechanically, and for the EM field):

> *The natural degrees of freedom of a large system are not its microscopic constituents but its collective, long-wavelength excitations. The bookkeeping device for these excitations is a field.*

### 1.1 Classical chain → continuum field

Take $N$ ions of mass $m$ on a ring, equilibrium spacing $a$, connected by springs $k_s$. Writing $R_I(t) = \bar R_I + \phi_I(t)$ for small displacements,

$$
L = \sum_{I=1}^{N}\left[\frac{m}{2}\dot\phi_I^2 - \frac{k_s}{2}\,(\phi_{I+1}-\phi_I)^2\right].
$$

Because we only care about physics on scales $\gg a$, we blur the index $I$ into a coordinate $x = Ia$ and the vector $\{\phi_I\}$ into a smooth function $\phi(x)$:

$$
\phi_I \to a^{1/2}\phi(x)\big|_{x=Ia},\qquad
\phi_{I+1}-\phi_I \to a^{3/2}\partial_x\phi,\qquad
\sum_I \to \frac{1}{a}\int_0^L dx .
$$

The result is a *field theory*,

$$
L[\phi]=\int_0^L dx\,\left[\frac{m}{2}\dot\phi^2 -\frac{k_s a^2}{2}(\partial_x\phi)^2\right],
\qquad
\big(m\,\partial_t^2 - k_s a^2\,\partial_x^2\big)\phi = 0,
$$

whose solutions are sound waves with velocity $v=a\sqrt{k_s/m}$. The elementary excitations are *collective*: a single wave involves all atoms at once.

### 1.2 Quantizing the chain: phonons and ladder operators

Promote $\phi(x),\ \pi(x)=m\dot\phi(x)$ to operators with $[\hat\pi(x),\hat\phi(x')]=-i\delta(x-x')$. Fourier transforming,

$$
\hat H=\sum_k\left[\frac{1}{2m}\hat\pi_k\hat\pi_{-k} +\frac{m\omega_k^2}{2}\hat\phi_k\hat\phi_{-k}\right],
\qquad \omega_k = v|k|,
$$

which is a stack of independent harmonic oscillators, one per mode $k$. Defining ladder operators $a_k, a_k^\dagger$ mode by mode,

$$
\hat H=\sum_k \omega_k\Big(a_k^\dagger a_k+\tfrac12\Big),
\qquad [a_k,a^\dagger_{k'}]=\delta_{kk'} .
\tag{1}
$$

The quanta counted by $a_k^\dagger a_k$ are **phonons**. Two lessons we carry forward:

- **(a)** Excitations of a many-body system behave like *particles created by operators*, and the entire physics is stored in the operator algebra $[a,a^\dagger]=1$, not in wavefunctions.
- **(b)** There are two natural, Fourier-conjugate descriptions: local ($\hat\phi(x)$, real space) and modal ($a_k$, momentum space). *This local ↔ modal duality is exactly the Bloch ↔ Wannier duality we are heading toward.*

# Part II — The story of Chapter 2: second quantization as a change-of-basis machine

## 2. What second quantization actually is

You mentioned the Dirac origin, and that framing is the right one to remember: Dirac (1927) noticed that if you take the Schrödinger *wavefunction* $\psi(x)$ and promote it to an *operator field* $a(x)$ obeying canonical (anti)commutation relations, then the resulting theory automatically describes an arbitrary number of identical, correctly (anti)symmetrized particles. Historically this looked like "quantizing a second time," hence the (misleading) name. Fundamentally, there is only one quantization: second quantization is a *representation* of many-body quantum mechanics in which the tedious (anti)symmetrization of Eq. (2.1) of the book is automated by operator algebra.

### 2.1 Fock space and the algebra

Instead of tracking a symmetrized $N$-particle wavefunction, track how many particles occupy each single-particle state $\lambda$: $|n_1,n_2,\dots\rangle$. Allowing all particle numbers at once defines **Fock space** $\mathcal F=\bigoplus_{N\ge 0}\mathcal F^N$, with a vacuum $|0\rangle$. Define

$$
a_\lambda^\dagger|\dots,n_\lambda,\dots\rangle
=(n_\lambda+1)^{1/2}\,\zeta^{s_\lambda}\,|\dots,n_\lambda+1,\dots\rangle,
\qquad \zeta=\begin{cases}+1 & \text{bosons}\\ -1 & \text{fermions,}\end{cases}
$$

and the whole theory collapses into three relations,

$$
[a_\lambda,a^\dagger_{\lambda'}]_\zeta=\delta_{\lambda\lambda'},\qquad
[a_\lambda,a_{\lambda'}]_\zeta=0,\qquad
[a^\dagger_\lambda,a^\dagger_{\lambda'}]_\zeta=0,
\tag{2}
$$

where $[\,\cdot,\cdot\,]_\zeta$ is the commutator (bosons) or anticommutator (fermions). Every basis state is $|n_1,n_2,\dots\rangle=\prod_\lambda (n_\lambda!)^{-1/2}(a^\dagger_\lambda)^{n_\lambda}|0\rangle$.

### 2.2 The one formula you must own: change of basis

For everything that follows, this is the crucial tool. If $\{|\lambda\rangle\}$ and $\{|\tilde\lambda\rangle\}$ are two orthonormal single-particle bases, then inserting a resolution of identity in $|\tilde\lambda\rangle=\sum_\lambda|\lambda\rangle\langle\lambda|\tilde\lambda\rangle$ and using $|\lambda\rangle=a^\dagger_\lambda|0\rangle$ gives

$$
\boxed{\;
a^\dagger_{\tilde\lambda}=\sum_\lambda \langle\lambda|\tilde\lambda\rangle\, a^\dagger_\lambda,
\qquad
a_{\tilde\lambda}=\sum_\lambda \langle\tilde\lambda|\lambda\rangle\, a_\lambda
\;}
\tag{3}
$$

If the single-particle transformation is *unitary*, the new operators automatically satisfy the same canonical algebra (2). Check it for fermions:

$$
\{a_{\tilde\lambda},a^\dagger_{\tilde\lambda'}\}
=\sum_{\lambda\lambda'}\langle\tilde\lambda|\lambda\rangle
\langle\lambda'|\tilde\lambda'\rangle\{a_\lambda,a^\dagger_{\lambda'}\}
=\langle\tilde\lambda|\tilde\lambda'\rangle=\delta_{\tilde\lambda\tilde\lambda'}.
$$

> **Key idea.** The Wannier construction is nothing more than a specific, physically motivated choice of unitary basis change (3): from Bloch momentum states to localized lattice-site states. All the subtlety of Wannier functions lives in the *single-particle* overlap $\langle\lambda|\tilde\lambda\rangle$; the many-body machinery comes along for free.

### 2.3 Operators in second quantization (one- and two-body)

One-body operators (kinetic energy, lattice potential):

$$
\hat{\mathcal O}_1=\sum_{\mu\nu}\langle\mu|\hat o|\nu\rangle\, a^\dagger_\mu a_\nu ,
\qquad\text{e.g.}\qquad
\hat H_0=\int d^dr\; a^\dagger(\mathbf r)\Big[\frac{\hat{\mathbf p}^2}{2m}+V(\mathbf r)\Big]a(\mathbf r).
\tag{4}
$$

Two-body operators (Coulomb):

$$
\hat V_{ee}=\frac12\int d^dr\,d^dr'\;
a^\dagger_\sigma(\mathbf r)a^\dagger_{\sigma'}(\mathbf r')\,
V(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')\,a_{\sigma'}(\mathbf r')a_\sigma(\mathbf r).
\tag{5}
$$

Chapter 2 then plays this machinery on three instruments: the nearly free electron gas (plane-wave basis natural), the tight-binding solid (*Wannier basis natural* — our target), and derived models (Hubbard, Heisenberg, Luttinger liquid). The moral of the chapter:

> **Physical picture.** Choosing the right single-particle basis is not cosmetic. In the plane-wave basis the Hubbard interaction looks hopelessly nonlocal; in the Wannier basis it collapses to $U\hat n_{i\uparrow}\hat n_{i\downarrow}$. The physics (localized electrons, atomic limit, Mott insulators, superexchange) dictates the basis. Wannier functions are "the basis the atomic limit wants."

*End of story mode.* We now know (i) what fields and ladder operators are, (ii) how basis changes act on them, (iii) why we want a localized basis. We are ready.

# Part III — Wannier functions, properly

## 3. Setting the stage: Bloch's theorem

Electrons in a crystal see a lattice-periodic potential $V(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=V(\mathbf r)$ for every lattice vector $\mathbf R$. In one dimension with lattice constant $a$: $V(x+a)=V(x)$.

> **Theorem (Bloch).** The eigenstates of $\hat H=\hat{\mathbf p}^2/2m+V(\mathbf r)$ can be chosen as
> $$
> \psi_{\mathbf k n}(\mathbf r)=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\,u_{\mathbf k n}(\mathbf r),
> \qquad u_{\mathbf k n}(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)=u_{\mathbf k n}(\mathbf r),
> \tag{6}
> $$
> with $\hat H\psi_{\mathbf k n}=\epsilon_{n}(\mathbf k)\,\psi_{\mathbf k n}$. Here $n$ is the *band index* and the crystal momentum $\mathbf k$ lives in the first Brillouin zone (BZ), $k_i\in[-\pi/a,\pi/a]$. For a finite crystal of $N$ cells with periodic boundary conditions, $\mathbf k$ takes $N$ discrete values per band.

Three facts to internalize before defining Wannier functions:

- **B1. Bloch states are maximally delocalized.** $|\psi_{\mathbf kn}(\mathbf r)|^2 = |u_{\mathbf kn}(\mathbf r)|^2$ is periodic: the electron is spread with equal weight over *every* unit cell. Perfect for band energies; useless for talking about "the electron on atom $i$."
- **B2. One band = one $N$-dimensional Hilbert space.** Fixing $n$ and letting $\mathbf k$ run over the BZ gives $N$ orthonormal states $\{|\psi_{\mathbf k n}\rangle\}$. They span a subspace $\mathcal H_n\subset\mathcal H$: the "band Hilbert space." Everything we do next happens *inside* $\mathcal H_n$ (or inside a chosen group of bands).
- **B3. Phase (gauge) freedom.** Eigenstates are defined only up to a phase: for each $\mathbf k$ separately,
  $$
  |\psi_{\mathbf k n}\rangle\;\longrightarrow\; e^{i\varphi_n(\mathbf k)}|\psi_{\mathbf k n}\rangle
  \tag{7}
  $$
  is an equally good Bloch state. This innocent-looking freedom will turn out to control *how localized* a Wannier function can be. Keep it in view.

## 4. Definition of the Wannier function

> **Definition (Wannier state).** For band $n$ and lattice site $\mathbf R$, define
> $$
> \boxed{\;
> |w_{\mathbf R n}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{\mathbf k\in\mathrm{BZ}} e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}\,|\psi_{\mathbf k n}\rangle
> \;}
> \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
> |\psi_{\mathbf k n}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{\mathbf R} e^{+i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}\,|w_{\mathbf R n}\rangle .
> \tag{8}
> $$
> The Wannier *function* is $w_{\mathbf Rn}(\mathbf r)=\langle\mathbf r|w_{\mathbf Rn}\rangle$. (This is Eq. (2.22) of Altland & Simons, with $|\psi_{\mathbf R n}\rangle\equiv|w_{\mathbf R n}\rangle$.)

Read it as plainly as possible: **a Wannier state is the discrete Fourier transform of a Bloch band, with the lattice vector $\mathbf R$ playing the role of the conjugate variable to the crystal momentum $\mathbf k$.** You said you are comfortable with Fourier transforms — then you already own Eq. (8). Everything else is unpacking consequences.

### 4.1 Why this is the natural "localizing" move

Recall the elementary fact: the Fourier transform of a broad, smooth function is narrow. A Bloch state is "narrow in $\mathbf k$" (a single $\mathbf k$) and therefore infinitely spread in $\mathbf r$. To build something localized in $\mathbf r$ we must superpose *all* $\mathbf k$'s of the band with equal weight — which is exactly what Eq. (8) does. The phase factor $e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}$ centers the resulting wave packet on cell $\mathbf R$.

There is a perfect analogy you already know:

| | delocalized / modal | localized |
|---|---|---|
| free space | plane waves $e^{ikx}$ | position states $\delta(x-x_0)$ |
| harmonic chain (Ch. 1) | phonon modes $a_k$ | local displacement $\hat\phi(x)$ |
| crystal band | Bloch states $\|\psi_{\mathbf kn}\rangle$ | **Wannier states** $\|w_{\mathbf Rn}\rangle$ |

Wannier states are "as close to position eigenstates as a single band allows." You cannot localize better than one band's worth of states permits — a band has only $N$ states, one per unit cell, so the sharpest possible real-space resolution is *one state per cell*. That is precisely what Wannier functions deliver.

### 4.2 Explicit structure of $w$

Insert the Bloch form (6):

$$
w_{\mathbf Rn}(\mathbf r)=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{\mathbf k}
e^{i\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf R)}\,u_{\mathbf kn}(\mathbf r).
\tag{9}
$$

Two immediate structural facts:

**(i) Translational covariance.** All Wannier functions of a band are copies of one another, rigidly translated:

$$
w_{\mathbf Rn}(\mathbf r)=w_{0 n}(\mathbf r-\mathbf R).
\tag{10}
$$

*Proof.* In (9) shift $\mathbf r\to\mathbf r+\mathbf R$ and use periodicity of $u_{\mathbf kn}$:

$$
w_{\mathbf Rn}(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)
=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{\mathbf k}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}u_{\mathbf kn}(\mathbf r+\mathbf R)
=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{\mathbf k}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}u_{\mathbf kn}(\mathbf r)
=w_{0n}(\mathbf r). \qquad\square
$$

So a band is characterized by a *single* function $w_{0n}$, "the" Wannier function of band $n$, stamped onto every cell.

**(ii) Atomic-limit intuition.** If the atoms are far apart (tight-binding/atomic limit), $u_{\mathbf kn}$ barely depends on $\mathbf k$, and $w_{0n}(\mathbf r)$ reduces to the $n$-th *atomic orbital* of the isolated atom at the origin. As atoms are brought together, the Wannier function remains atomic-orbital-like near its center but grows small oscillating tails in the neighboring cells. Those tails are not noise — they are exactly what enforces orthogonality between neighboring Wannier functions (next section).

## 5. Orthonormality and completeness (the "rigid" properties)

Everything in this section is gauge-independent and exact. The only tool is the lattice sum identity

$$
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf k\in\mathrm{BZ}} e^{i\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf R-\mathbf R')}
=\delta_{\mathbf R,\mathbf R'},
\qquad
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf R} e^{i(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')\cdot\mathbf R}
=\delta_{\mathbf k,\mathbf k'},
\tag{11}
$$

the discrete analog of $\int\frac{dk}{2\pi}e^{ik(x-x')}=\delta(x-x')$.

**Claim 1 (Orthonormality).** $\langle w_{\mathbf Rn}|w_{\mathbf R'n'}\rangle=\delta_{\mathbf R\mathbf R'}\,\delta_{nn'}$.

*Proof.* Direct computation using Bloch orthonormality $\langle\psi_{\mathbf kn}|\psi_{\mathbf k'n'}\rangle=\delta_{\mathbf k\mathbf k'}\delta_{nn'}$:

$$
\langle w_{\mathbf Rn}|w_{\mathbf R'n'}\rangle
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf k\mathbf k'}e^{+i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}e^{-i\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf R'}
\langle\psi_{\mathbf kn}|\psi_{\mathbf k'n'}\rangle
=\frac{\delta_{nn'}}{N}\sum_{\mathbf k}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf R-\mathbf R')}
=\delta_{nn'}\,\delta_{\mathbf R\mathbf R'}. \qquad\square
$$

**Claim 2 (Completeness within the band(s)).**

$$
\sum_{\mathbf R}|w_{\mathbf Rn}\rangle\langle w_{\mathbf Rn}|
=\sum_{\mathbf k\in\mathrm{BZ}}|\psi_{\mathbf kn}\rangle\langle\psi_{\mathbf kn}|
\equiv \hat P_n,
$$

the projector onto band $n$. Summing over all bands, $\sum_{n\mathbf R}|w_{\mathbf Rn}\rangle\langle w_{\mathbf Rn}|=\mathbb 1$.

*Proof.* Insert the definition and use (11) on the $\mathbf R$-sum:

$$
\sum_{\mathbf R}|w_{\mathbf Rn}\rangle\langle w_{\mathbf Rn}|
=\frac1N\sum_{\mathbf R}\sum_{\mathbf k\mathbf k'}e^{i(\mathbf k'-\mathbf k)\cdot\mathbf R}
|\psi_{\mathbf kn}\rangle\langle\psi_{\mathbf k'n}|
=\sum_{\mathbf k}|\psi_{\mathbf kn}\rangle\langle\psi_{\mathbf kn}|. \qquad\square
$$

> **⚠ Careful: "overcomplete" is the wrong word.**
> You wrote that the Wannier basis should be "localized and overcomplete." If that phrasing came from memory, it is worth correcting before it settles: the Wannier set $\{|w_{\mathbf Rn}\rangle\}$ is **orthonormal and complete** — exactly one state per unit cell per band, no redundancy, as the two claims above show. It is *not* overcomplete.
>
> Where might the word "overcomplete" have come from? Two legitimate nearby notions, easy to conflate with Wannier functions:
>
> - **Coherent states** (and Gaussian wave packets generally) form genuinely overcomplete, non-orthogonal families. Wannier functions are the lattice-adapted way to *avoid* paying the price of overcompleteness while still being localized.
> - **Non-orthogonal atomic orbitals**: if instead of Wannier functions you use bare atomic orbitals $\phi_{\mathrm{at}}(\mathbf r-\mathbf R)$ centered on each site, neighboring orbitals overlap, $S_{\mathbf R\mathbf R'}\neq\delta_{\mathbf R\mathbf R'}$. That basis is complete but *non-orthogonal* (and in some treatments padded into overcomplete sets). The Wannier construction is precisely the canonical orthogonalization of that idea: it keeps the localization but restores $S=\mathbb 1$, at the cost of the small oscillating tails mentioned above.
>
> If, on the other hand, "overcomplete" referred to keeping *several bands* of Wannier functions when only one band is occupied — that is not overcompleteness either, just a larger (still orthonormal) basis. For clean second quantization, Eq. (2), orthonormality is what you want: it is what guarantees $\{c_i,c_j^\dagger\}=\delta_{ij}$ for the site operators of any lattice fermion model.

## 6. Second quantization in the Wannier basis

Now we cash in the machinery of Part II. Apply the basis-change rule (3) with $|\tilde\lambda\rangle=|w_{\mathbf R_i n}\rangle$ and $|\lambda\rangle=|\psi_{\mathbf kn}\rangle$; the overlaps are pure phases, $\langle\psi_{\mathbf kn}|w_{\mathbf R_i n}\rangle=e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R_i}/\sqrt N$. Hence (suppressing the band index, working within one band, and writing $a_{i\sigma}^\dagger$ for the operator creating an electron in $|w_{\mathbf R_i}\rangle$ with spin $\sigma$):

$$
a^\dagger_{i\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{\mathbf k\in\mathrm{BZ}}
e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R_i}\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf k\sigma},
\qquad
a^\dagger_{\mathbf k\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{i}
e^{+i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R_i}\,a^\dagger_{i\sigma}.
\tag{12}
$$

This is Eq. (2.24) of the book. Because the transformation is unitary, the Wannier-site operators inherit canonical anticommutation relations,

$$
\{a_{i\sigma},a^\dagger_{j\sigma'}\}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{\sigma\sigma'},
$$

which is the algebra you use, without comment, every time you write a lattice model.

### 6.1 The kinetic term becomes hopping

The Bloch basis diagonalizes $\hat H_0$: $\hat H_0=\sum_{\mathbf k}\epsilon_{\mathbf k}\,a^\dagger_{\mathbf k\sigma}a_{\mathbf k\sigma}$. Substituting (12),

$$
\hat H_0
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{ij}\sum_{\mathbf k}
e^{i\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf R_i-\mathbf R_j)}\,\epsilon_{\mathbf k}\;
a^\dagger_{i\sigma}a_{j\sigma}
\;\equiv\;\sum_{ij} t_{ij}\; a^\dagger_{i\sigma}a_{j\sigma},
\qquad
\boxed{\;t_{ij}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf k}
e^{i\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf R_i-\mathbf R_j)}\,\epsilon_{\mathbf k}\;}
\tag{13}
$$

> **Physical picture.** The hopping amplitude $t_{ij}$ is the Fourier transform of the band dispersion. Smooth dispersion ⇔ rapidly decaying hopping. The Wannier basis converts "one particle with energy $\epsilon_{\mathbf k}$" into "a particle that lives on sites and tunnels between them." This is the *derivation* of the tight-binding model — not an ansatz.

**Example (sanity check in 1D).** Suppose the exact band of some 1D crystal happens to be $\epsilon_k=-2t\cos(ka)$. Then

$$
t_{ij}=\frac1N\sum_k e^{ik(R_i-R_j)}\big(-t\,e^{ika}-t\,e^{-ika}\big)
=-t\big(\delta_{R_i-R_j,\,-a}+\delta_{R_i-R_j,\,+a}\big),
$$

using (11): pure nearest-neighbor hopping of amplitude $-t$, as it must. Conversely, longer-range Fourier components of $\epsilon_k$ generate longer-range hoppings, with weights falling off as fast as the smoothness of $\epsilon_k$ allows. In the extreme atomic limit $\epsilon_{\mathbf k}=\mathrm{const}$, Eq. (13) gives $t_{ij}\propto\delta_{ij}$: no transport, isolated atoms.

### 6.2 Interactions: from Eq. (5) to Hubbard

Transforming the two-body interaction (5) to the Wannier basis using $a^\dagger_\sigma(\mathbf r)=\sum_i w^*_{\mathbf R_i}(\mathbf r)\,a^\dagger_{i\sigma}$ gives

$$
\hat V_{ee}=\sum_{ii'jj'}U_{ii'jj'}\,
a^\dagger_{i\sigma}a^\dagger_{i'\sigma'}a_{j'\sigma'}a_{j\sigma},
\qquad
U_{ii'jj'}=\frac12\int d^dr\, d^dr'\;
w^*_{\mathbf R_i}(\mathbf r)\,w_{\mathbf R_j}(\mathbf r)\,V(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')\,
w^*_{\mathbf R_{i'}}(\mathbf r')\,w_{\mathbf R_{j'}}(\mathbf r').
$$

Because Wannier functions are localized, the matrix elements are organized by distance, and in the atomic limit the on-site term dominates: $U_{iiii}\equiv U/2$. Keeping nearest-neighbor hopping and on-site repulsion:

$$
\hat H=-t\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}a^\dagger_{i\sigma}a_{j\sigma}
+U\sum_i \hat n_{i\uparrow}\hat n_{i\downarrow}
\qquad\text{(Hubbard model).}
$$

Subleading Wannier matrix elements generate, in controlled order: density-density terms $V_{ii'}\hat n_i\hat n_{i'}$ (charge-density-wave tendencies), ferromagnetic direct exchange $J^F_{ij}$, and — via second-order virtual hopping at half filling — antiferromagnetic superexchange $J=4t^2/U$ and the Heisenberg model. *The entire strong-correlation program of Ch. 2 is the physics of Wannier matrix elements.*

## 7. Localization: the delicate property

Orthonormality and completeness were rigid. Localization is the subtle part, and the part most worth understanding deeply.

### 7.1 Smoothness in $\mathbf k$ controls decay in $\mathbf r$

Write the Wannier function as (continuum limit of (9), 1D for clarity)

$$
w_0(x)=\sqrt{\frac{a}{2\pi}}\int_{-\pi/a}^{\pi/a} dk\; e^{ikx}\,u_k(x).
$$

This is a Fourier integral over a *compact* domain (the BZ, which is a circle, since $k=-\pi/a$ and $k=+\pi/a$ are identified). Standard Fourier analysis then dictates:

| regularity of $u_k$ in $k$ (as a map on the BZ circle) | decay of $w_0(x)$ |
|---|---|
| discontinuous | $\sim 1/x$ (bad) |
| $m$ times differentiable | $\sim 1/x^{m+1}$ |
| analytic in a strip $\lvert\Im k\rvert<\kappa$ | $\sim e^{-\kappa\lvert x\rvert}$ **(exponential)** |

> **Theorem (Kohn 1959; des Cloizeaux; Brouder et al.).** For an *isolated* band (separated from all other bands by a gap everywhere in the BZ), the Bloch functions can be chosen analytic in $\mathbf k$, and the resulting Wannier functions decay *exponentially*, $|w_0(\mathbf r)|\lesssim e^{-|\mathbf r|/\xi}$, with $\xi$ set by the gap (smaller gap ⇒ larger $\xi$). In 1D, with a natural reality/symmetry choice, the maximally localized Wannier function is unique and real.

> **Physical picture.** The band gap is what pays for localization. A Wannier function is a wave packet built strictly from one band; squeezing it in real space costs mixing-in of other-band character, which the gap forbids at low energy. Hence: isolated band ⇒ exponentially localized "effective orbital." As a gap closes, $\xi$ diverges and the local-orbital picture degrades — which is also the intuitive reason gapless/critical points resist tight-binding caricatures.

### 7.2 Gauge freedom: why "the" Wannier function is a choice

Here is where the phase freedom (7) strikes. Replace $|\psi_k\rangle\to e^{i\varphi(k)}|\psi_k\rangle$ in (8): you obtain a *different* set of Wannier functions — still orthonormal, still complete, still translationally covariant, but with different shapes and different spreads. Rough $\varphi(k)$ ruins localization; smart $\varphi(k)$ optimizes it.

The modern, constructive resolution (Marzari–Vanderbilt 1997): choose the gauge that minimizes the total quadratic spread

$$
\Omega=\sum_n\Big[\langle w_{0n}|\mathbf r^2|w_{0n}\rangle-\langle w_{0n}|\mathbf r|w_{0n}\rangle^2\Big]
\;\longrightarrow\;\min,
$$

defining **maximally localized Wannier functions (MLWFs)**. Two useful facts from that theory:

- The Wannier *centers* $\bar{\mathbf r}_n=\langle w_{0n}|\mathbf r|w_{0n}\rangle$ are gauge invariant modulo a lattice vector, and are given by the Berry phase of the band, $\bar r_n=\frac{a}{2\pi}\oint_{\mathrm{BZ}}dk\, i\langle\partial_k u_{kn}|u_{kn}\rangle$ (in 1D: the Zak phase). Wannier centers are thus *topological data*: in a topologically nontrivial band they can be obstructed from sitting where the atoms are — and for a Chern band in 2D, exponentially localized Wannier functions fail to exist altogether. (This is the band-theory face of the same bulk topology that underlies topological quantum computation platforms.)
- For a group of $J$ bands that are entangled among themselves but isolated from the rest, one generalizes (7) to a $k$-dependent $U(J)$ mixing, $|\psi_{k n}\rangle\to\sum_{m}U_{mn}(k)|\psi_{k m}\rangle$, and minimizes $\Omega$ over all such gauges: "composite-band" Wannier functions.

> **Remark (the three-way trade-off).** Within one band you can have any two of {perfect $\mathbf k$-space character, perfect localization, no gauge ambiguity} but not all three. Bloch states: sharp $\mathbf k$, zero localization. Wannier states: give up sharp $\mathbf k$ entirely, gain localization, and inherit a gauge choice which you then fix by a localization criterion.

## 8. Worked microcosm: two-site "crystal"

The smallest example that exhibits everything. Take $N=2$ sites with atomic orbitals $|1\rangle,|2\rangle$ and Hamiltonian $\hat H=-t(|1\rangle\langle 2|+|2\rangle\langle 1|)$. "Bloch momenta" are $k=0,\pi$ (in units of $1/a$), and the Bloch eigenstates are

$$
|\psi_{k=0}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle+|2\rangle),\quad \epsilon_0=-t;
\qquad
|\psi_{k=\pi}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle-|2\rangle),\quad \epsilon_\pi=+t.
$$

This toy has one band with two $k$ points. Apply (8):

$$
|w_{R_1}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(e^{-i0\cdot R_1}|\psi_0\rangle+e^{-i\pi R_1/a}|\psi_\pi\rangle\big)
=\tfrac12\big[(|1\rangle+|2\rangle)+(|1\rangle-|2\rangle)\big]=|1\rangle,
$$

$$
|w_{R_2}\rangle=\tfrac12\big[(|1\rangle+|2\rangle)-(|1\rangle-|2\rangle)\big]=|2\rangle.
$$

The Wannier transform *exactly undoes* the diagonalization and returns the localized orbitals — and (13) returns $t_{12}=\frac12(e^{i\cdot 0}\epsilon_0+e^{i\pi}\epsilon_\pi)=-t$. In a real crystal the same procedure works, except the returned local orbitals are not the bare atomic ones but their orthogonalized, gap-protected, exponentially localized descendants.

**Exercise.** Repeat with an on-site staggering $\pm\Delta$ added ($\hat H\to\hat H+\Delta(|1\rangle\langle1|-|2\rangle\langle2|)$) and watch the Wannier functions acquire weight on both sites. Then take the $N$-site Su–Schrieffer–Heeger chain and compute the Wannier center as a function of the dimerization sign: you will see the Zak phase / topological obstruction of Sec. 7 concretely.

## 9. Wannier functions in your research context (monitored lattice fermions)

A short, carefully hedged bridge — take what is useful, discard what is not.

- In lattice models of monitored/adaptive fermion circuits, one writes $L$ complex fermion sites $c_i,c_i^\dagger$ (or $2L$ Majoranas $\gamma_{2i-1},\gamma_{2i}$) with $\{c_i,c_j^\dagger\}=\delta_{ij}$, and measures/evolves *local* operators (on-site parities, nearest-neighbor tunneling/pairing terms). If those lattice modes are regarded as arising from an underlying continuum band structure, then the "site" operators are Wannier-basis operators in the sense of (12), and the locality of the measured operators is meaningful precisely because the underlying single-particle basis is *localized and orthonormal*. If instead the model is defined directly on the lattice (as in the class-DIII / class-D circuits you work with), the Wannier story is the reason such lattice definitions faithfully caricature real fermionic matter.
- Orthonormality is what makes the Gaussian/covariance-matrix formalism tick: $\Gamma_{ab}\propto\langle[\gamma_a,\gamma_b]\rangle$ presupposes a canonical Majorana algebra, which under any *unitary* single-particle basis change (Sec. 2.2) is preserved. A non-orthogonal (let alone overcomplete) local basis would wreck the simple algebra $\{\gamma_a,\gamma_b\}=2\delta_{ab}$ and with it the covariance-matrix update rules.
- The gap–localization theorem gives a useful instinct for measurement-induced criticality: away from criticality one expects effective local (Wannier-like) descriptions of the steady state to work well, while at a critical point (vanishing gap / diverging correlation length) strictly local mode descriptions become long-ranged — the single-particle analog of the entanglement-structure change across the transition. This is offered as an intuition pump, not a theorem about your specific circuits.

## Appendix A: continuum-limit conventions

For an infinite crystal one trades $\frac1N\sum_{\mathbf k}\to \frac{v_c}{(2\pi)^d}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}d^dk$ (with $v_c$ the unit-cell volume) and the Wannier function reads

$$
w_{n}(\mathbf r-\mathbf R)=\frac{v_c}{(2\pi)^d}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}d^dk\;
e^{i\mathbf k\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf R)}\,u_{\mathbf kn}(\mathbf r),
$$

with orthonormality $\int d^dr\, w^*_n(\mathbf r-\mathbf R)\,w_{n'}(\mathbf r-\mathbf R')=\delta_{nn'}\delta_{\mathbf R\mathbf R'}$ intact.
